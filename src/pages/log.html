The first video made from the app, end to end

A one-line brief typed in Studio became a 30-second film with nobody stepping in: 4 seconds of preflight, 5 minutes 24 of exploration in 42 browser actions, a four-section plan, 18 minutes of filming at 2x on a laptop, then the edit and the checks. 29 minutes in all.

The checks flagged two things, and they were right. The film came out at half the length asked for, and the last line was still being spoken when the picture ended. Both belong to the planner, and both are on the list.

The app, rebuilt around a plan

The storyboard step, the render page and the editor for finished videos are gone. In their place is one window per video: the script as a document, the picture as a canvas where everything is dragged, the settings, and a timeline to scale. It is where a video is planned, where it is generated, and where every later version is made.

One thing survived from the old editor, because it was the best part: pointing at the picture to say what should change.

A launch video is not a demo

Three templates in a day: Studio, Dynamic Demo and Launch Film. The third one started life under the wrong name. A film that walks through a product step by step is a demo, whatever the camera does.

A launch film argues. It starts from answers, not from a recording: who it is for, what today costs them in hours or money, one claim, three proofs, one action. The product is on screen for seconds, behind a sentence. Its music is written in code, so it is cut to the picture.

The first template, measured before it was built

Mac Demo copies the look of the best desktop screen recordings, so 17 reference videos were measured first: two camera states and nothing in between, a spring near 7 rad/s on zoom and position together, a pointer more than three times the system size.

Then the render was put through the same instrument. Reference zoom 2.25, ours 2.25. Reference spring 6.6 to 7.0 rad/s, ours 7.05. The first film of a live site took 2 minutes to explore, 22 to film and 5 to render.

It's called Framefly now

The working name had to go. Framefly is what the product does: an agent that moves through your app like a fly on the wall, and turns what it sees into frames. The key-light lime the interface was already built around turned out to be the colour of a firefly.

The new mark is a viewfinder locked on a subject, with its flight path drawn as onion-skinned frames. Same day: a launch date, October 28, and this site.

Why Framefly doesn't screen-record

The same 20-second scenario was captured five ways. Every real-time method stuttered: 20 to 34 unique frames per second, with freezes of up to 0.9 seconds, because the browser renders and encodes on the same CPU the page needs. Real-time capture also quietly dropped 2x density back to 1x.

Frame-by-frame capture fixed it: virtual clocks and one rendered frame per 1/60 s of page time gave 55 unique frames per second, no freeze longer than one frame, and true 3200x1800 frames. The first attempt came out with a blank main area, because CSS animations froze with the clock. The fix steps every running animation forward on each frame.

The agent's first real exploration

Pointed at the Framefly interface itself, the agent mapped six screens, ranked them by importance, and wrote a six-section plan of about 46 seconds with voice-over lines and more than 40 replayable steps. It took 5 minutes 39 seconds.

Along the way it found two of its own broken selectors by replaying its plan, and fixed them. It refused every destructive or financial button and listed each one. Its weak spot: it walked back and forth between screens instead of one continuous path, which wastes camera time.

Timing comes from real speech

Plans first assumed narration at 150 words a minute. Real synthesized speech ran at about 190. So section lengths now come from the generated audio, never from an estimate, and the voice is generated before a single frame is filmed.

The voice leaderboards also moved this year, so the provider will be picked by ear: the same six lines through four candidates, labels hidden.
